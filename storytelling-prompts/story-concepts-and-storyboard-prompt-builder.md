# Story Concepts & Storyboard Prompt Builder

Paste this whole thing to Claude (or any AI assistant) as-is — don't fill in anything yourself first. The AI will ask you questions, then brainstorm three distinct visual story concepts and turn each into an image prompt ready for storyboarding.

This builds on the same story framework as the Visual Story & Image Prompt Builder (protagonist, conflict, surprise, symbolism, color, composition, style, typography) — but instead of developing one concept in depth, the goal here is to generate three genuinely different directions so a client can compare and choose.

---

You are my design storytelling coach. Help me brainstorm three distinct visual story concepts for a design project, each using a different creative approach, then turn each into an image prompt suitable for a storyboard or mood board.

**Step 1 — Ask me these questions first, one at a time, and wait for my answers:**

1. What's the project, product, or campaign you're designing for?
2. Who's the target audience, and what preconceived notions, resistance, or indifference might they already have about this topic?
3. What's a realistic, measurable short-term goal for this piece — something small and observable, not a lofty long-term outcome? (e.g., "more people ask a follow-up question" rather than "changes someone's career path"). If you're not sure, describe the big-picture goal you're hoping for and I'll help you narrow it down to something doable.
4. Who do you think the protagonist of this story is — the brand, the product, the viewer themselves, or something else entirely? Remember: the protagonist is whoever "the events of the story mean the most to," and it's often not the person pictured in the image. If you're not sure, I'll explore different protagonist angles across the three concepts.
5. What's the opposing force, danger, or problem this protagonist faces — or that your product/idea helps them avoid or defeat? Think: what is the predator in the bushes? If you're not sure, describe the benefit or outcome you want to convey and I'll help find the tension underneath it.
6. Is there a gatekeeper — someone who has to choose to use, display, or distribute this design before it ever reaches the end audience? (e.g., a store buyer, a guidance counselor, an editor, a client who has to approve and champion it internally.) If yes: what would need to be true about a concept for that person — in the middle of their chaotic day — to feel genuinely motivated to use it? A concept that the end audience would love but that the gatekeeper finds forgettable will never reach anyone.
7. Do you already have any early concept ideas in mind, even rough ones? If yes, briefly describe them so I can develop them further. If no, I'll generate three from scratch based on your answers above.
8. For the final deliverable, do you want mood boards (a looser collage of image, color, and type direction) or rough story comps (a small sequence of frames, like a mini storyboard)?

**Step 2 — Once I've answered, brainstorm three concepts that are meaningfully distinct from one another** — not three versions of the same idea, but three different creative strategies. One strong way to ensure genuine difference: give each concept a different protagonist type (e.g. one where the brand is the hero, one where the viewer is the protagonist and the characters are placeholders for them, one where the product itself faces the conflict). Classic creative strategies to draw from:

- Threat before it lands → protagonist is unaware; the viewer sees the danger coming (suspense or dark humour)
- Object lesson → show the amusing or dire result of NOT using the product ("don't let this happen to you")
- Viewer as protagonist → characters in the image are deliberate stand-ins; the viewer projects themselves into the scene
- Product as unlikely hero → the object itself bravely withstands whatever life throws at it
- Flip an assumption → take the most obvious expectation and invert it for a comedic or poignant surprise (e.g., "science is boring" shown next to hazmat suits and explosions in action-movie style)
- Aspirational reveal → a quiet emotional moment where the viewer realises what's possible (e.g., a dreamy, candy-coloured image of a child imagining themselves as a trailblazer)
- Before/after transformation → two states that let the viewer fill in the story between them (e.g., gritty kid on the left, successful doctor on the right — "it's the tough ones that make the best doctors")
- Target the skeptics, not the champions → aim at the resistant majority who don't see themselves as your audience, rather than the already-converted; the people who think this isn't for them are often the most powerful audience if you can reach them

**Symbolism note:** don't dismiss a symbol because it feels too obvious or familiar. Obvious symbols work precisely because familiarity is what makes an aha moment possible — the viewer needs to recognise the shorthand instantly before they can feel the twist.

For each concept, give me:

- A one-line concept summary and why it has "a glimmer of excitement" — a quick reason it could resonate, even if it's not fully polished yet. Also note: would the gatekeeper from question 6 feel motivated to use this — or would it require too much effort or feel too risky for them?
- The story elements: who or what the protagonist is, what conflict or tension it plays on, what kind of surprise it uses (humor, poignancy, or suspense), and — critically — name the specific **aha/get-it moment**: the small intellectual gap the viewer closes themselves, because that moment of realisation is what makes the story stick
- The visual toolbox choices: symbolism, color/mood, composition approach, style/era reference, and how typography or a headline plays a role (including which word is the "protagonist word" — the one the reader must see first and feel most)
- A storyboard-ready image prompt — if I asked for mood boards, one detailed prompt capturing the concept's key visual and mood; if I asked for story comps, a short prompt for each frame in the sequence

**Step 3 — After presenting all three, give me a one-paragraph note on how the three concepts differ from each other** — covering the protagonist type, the creative strategy, the surprise flavor, and the overall emotional register — so it's easy to see at a glance why each one is a genuinely distinct option rather than a variation on a theme.
