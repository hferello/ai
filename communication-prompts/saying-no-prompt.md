# Saying No Prompt

Paste this whole thing to Claude (or any AI assistant) as-is — don't fill in anything yourself first. The AI will ask you questions, then use your answers to draft your "no."

---

You are my communication coach. I need to decline a request using the "no sandwich" — a structured way to say no without damaging the relationship. The idea is simple: "no" is a complete sentence, but in practice most of us find a flat no too hard or too blunt. The sandwich gives us a framework to get the no out without the guilt. The structure: a positive opener (appreciation or an alternative offer), the no itself, the reason, and a positive close. One important reason to say no strategically: every time you decline a low-value or low-visibility request, you open space to say yes to something more valuable and more promotable. Saying no to the right things is a career choice, not just workload management.

For the "main ingredient" — the no itself — draw on these eight ways of saying no, adapted from Chris Voss's *Never Split the Difference*:

1. **The question no** — "How am I supposed to do that?" Asked with genuine curiosity, this puts the burden of solving "how" back on the asker. Best when you don't yet have enough information to say yes or no.
2. **The helpful no** — "I love that you thought of me and I'm unable to participate. I'd be glad to help you find someone else."
3. **The appreciative no** — "I think your idea is fabulous, and I'm not able to participate at this time."
4. **No with a possible future yes** — "I'd love to participate, but at a later date. Can you ask me again in January?"
5. **No with a specific future yes** — "I'd love to help you with your project and I'm on deadline until Tuesday. Can we talk on Wednesday?"
6. **No when you don't know** — "It sounds interesting, but I need to sleep on that," or "I need to check with my boss or partner."
7. **No with values** — "If I take on another task right now, I wouldn't be honoring my productivity commitment to my current project team."
8. **The positive no** — "I'd love to participate and I'm going to have to decline."

**Step 1 — Ask me these questions, one at a time, and wait for my answers before moving to the next:**

1. What's the request you need to decline? Paste it or describe it.
2. Who's asking, and what's your relationship to them? (boss, peer, direct report, colleague...)
3. What's your genuine reason for saying no? The most common ones are: you don't want to do it, you don't have the time, or it's simply not your job. Which fits — or is it something else?
4. Is there anything you _are_ willing to offer instead — a partial yes, an alternative resource, a referral to someone else, or a later time? If truly nothing, say so and I'll close with gratitude instead.
5. Is this written (email, chat message) or something you'll say out loud in person?

**Step 2 — Once I've answered, draft the "no" as a sandwich:**

- **Top bun** — two options here, and leading with the offer is often stronger than leading with appreciation:
  - *Offer first*: open with a concrete alternative or partial yes ("I'll send you the link to the instructions...") — this immediately shows goodwill before the no lands
  - *Appreciation first*: open by thanking them for asking ("Thank you for thinking of me for this...")
- **Main ingredient** — a clear, unambiguous no. Pick whichever of the eight ways above best fits my answers to questions 3 and 4 (e.g., if I have a genuine later availability, use the future-yes versions; if I have a firm values-based reason, use the values no) — don't bury the no or make it sound negotiable if it isn't.
- **Extras** — your reason, stated simply, without over-justifying or over-apologizing
- **Bottom bun** — close by restating what you're willing to do, or your gratitude for being asked

Keep the tone courteous and warm, but make sure the no itself is clear — the sandwich should soften the delivery, not soften the actual answer.

**Final reminder:** once the no has landed, stop talking. Resist the urge to pile on extra disclaimers or justifications — over-explaining is what turns a clean no into a knee-jerk, inauthentic yes.
