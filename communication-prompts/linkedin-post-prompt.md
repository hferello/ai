## What actually works on LinkedIn (apply these rules to everything you write)

**Format:**
- Native document carousels (PDF uploads) get the highest engagement (~7%). Flag when a topic would work better as a carousel than a text post.
- Multi-image posts (~6.8%) work well for project stories with real screenshots.
- Strong text-only posts (~4.3%) can outperform everything when the writing is genuinely good.
- Never put external links in the post body — they cut reach by 40–60%. Put links in the first comment instead.

**Algorithm signals (highest to lowest weight):**
Saves → DM shares → Comments → Dwell time → Likes

Comments carry 15× the algorithmic weight of a like. Every post needs an ending that earns a real response, not a nod.

**The hook (first ~140 characters) is 80% of the outcome:**
- Stat hook: a specific number that creates curiosity about the source (1.67× lift)
- Story hook: an opening that triggers "what happens next" (1.51× lift)
- Contrarian hook: challenges a widely-held belief (high lift, context-dependent)
- Curiosity gap: names a thing without revealing it yet
- FOMO / anxiety hook: surfaces a fear the reader already carries — that they're doing it wrong, missing what their peers know, or about to be caught out by a client, boss, or their own future self. Powerful when it names a real professional insecurity, not manufactured panic. Often the flip side of an aspiration ("the skill that gets you in the room")
- Never use imperative hooks ("Stop doing X", "Here's what nobody tells you") — they're overused to the point of invisibility

**The hook must be an OPEN loop, not a closed one (the #1 reason hooks fall flat):**

A complete, impressive statement still fails as a hook because it *answers* instead of *asks*. Once the reader's brain registers the fact, the loop closes and there's no pull to tap "see more." Impressive ≠ curious. The job of the hook is not to be the most impressive line — it's to leave something deliberately unresolved.

The mechanism is the curiosity gap (Zeigarnik effect): an open loop creates tension the brain wants to close, and the only way to close it is to keep reading.

**The formula:**

`[concrete, verifiable detail]  +  [a deliberately unresolved second beat]`

- The concrete detail earns trust (a number, a name, a place).
- The unresolved beat earns the tap (a tension, a contradiction, a "but", a thing named-but-not-explained).
- Dry hooks are all beat one and no beat two.

**Worked example** — turning a closed loop into an open one:

- ❌ Closed (dry): "I designed the screen system used across 270 London Underground stations."
  - Impressive, but fully resolved. Nothing to chase.
- ⚠️ Tease (weak open loop): "I designed the screen system used across 270 London Underground stations. You'd be surprised what I also designed…"
  - Opens a loop, but "you'd be surprised" *labels* the curiosity instead of *creating* it, and the trailing "…" reads as bait (LinkedIn already truncates with "see more").
- ✅ Strong open loop (the surprise is built into the content, via tension/contradiction):
  - "I designed the screen system used across 270 London Underground stations. It's not the work I'm proudest of." (contradiction → what is, then?)
  - "I designed the screen system used across 270 London Underground stations. Most people standing under it have no idea it was designed at all." (reframes into a point of view)
  - "I designed the screen system used across 270 London Underground stations. The hardest thing I built came this year, alone." (bridges to the real subject)

**An optional third beat — the payoff promise (use on how-to / resource posts):**

For posts that teach or hand over something, you can add a short forward-looking promise after the open loop. It tells the reader what they walk away with, which earns the tap on top of the curiosity. It must be plain and specific, never generic instruction-speak.

`[concrete detail]  +  [unresolved beat]  +  [plain payoff promise]`

- ✅ "...That was just the tip of it. This is what it takes to get in the room."
- ✅ "...It exposes it. Find out in 30 seconds."
- ✅ "...build the wrong thing faster. How to get out of this vibe coding loop:"
- ❌ "...Here's how to get better at design." (generic lesson, and "Here's how" is overused to invisibility)

Rules for the payoff beat:
- Vary the wording. Do not lean on "Here's how…" — it's become wallpaper. Reach for "This is what it takes", "Find out in 30 seconds", "The fix wasn't where anyone was looking".
- Avoid reusing "here" and "how" across posts. Repetition makes a set of hooks read as a template.
- Promise a *specific* payoff, not a vague lesson. "What it takes to X" beats "how to be better".

**Rules of thumb:**
- Tension reads as a human thought; a tease reads as marketing. Prefer a real contradiction or "but" over "you won't believe…".
- Don't make the label do the work the content should ("you'd be surprised", "this is wild"). Let a concrete detail create the gap.
- Skip the trailing "…". A full stop on an unresolved idea lands cleaner than an ellipsis.
- Always ask of any hook: *what am I deliberately not saying yet?* If the answer is "nothing", it's a closed loop — rewrite it.
- Keep the loop open *inside the preview*. The "see more" cut lands ~140 chars on mobile. If your second line resolves the tension above the fold, there's no reason to tap. Blank lines also eat preview space — keep the unresolved beat visible.
- If the post has an image or carousel, the hook should complement the visual, not repeat it. Let the image carry the concept and the text carry a concrete, specific beat (a square-wheel bike slide pairs with a stat, not with the word "iceberg").
- Anchor every stat in a real, verifiable source — never invent a number for impact. If the provenance is shaky, hedge ("industry estimates put it at…") and put the source in the first comment.
- A before/after pair of real numbers is its own strong open loop ("88% dropped off at install. Six months later, 300,000 new users a month."). The gap between the two numbers *is* the curiosity.

**Post length:** 900–1,300 characters is the sweet spot for text posts.

**Formatting rules:**
- One idea per paragraph. Maximum 2–3 sentences.
- Blank line between every paragraph. LinkedIn readers skim.
- The "see more" cutoff lands around line 3 on mobile. Make sure the hook ends on a cliffhanger or unresolved tension — not mid-thought.
- No bullet-pointed lists in the body. Use line breaks for rhythm instead.
- No filler transitions: "That being said", "With that in mind", "At the end of the day"
- No performative language: "I'm thrilled to share", "Excited to announce", "This changed my life"
- Never end with "Follow me for more" or "Repost if you agree"

**What drives consulting and job opportunities:**
- Posts that name a real problem the reader recognises in their own work
- Outcome-first stories (what changed, then how you did it)
- Counter-intuitive findings backed by evidence
- "I built/did something difficult" stories that show capability without stating it
- Teaching something genuinely useful with no ask attached

**Content themes by engagement rate:**
Personal story / vulnerability → 5.2%
Career lessons / failures → 4.8%
Industry hot takes → 4.3%
Data / research findings → 3.9%
How-to / educational → 3.1%
Company / product updates → 2.4%

## Tell me about yourself

Answer the questions below. The more specific you are, the better the posts.

**Your work:**
- What do you do, and for whom?
- What industry / discipline?
- What are you best known for among people who've worked with you?

**Your experience:**
- What are the 3–5 most interesting or significant projects you've worked on?
- For each: what was the real problem, what did you actually do, what changed because of it?
- Any metrics or outcomes you can share?

**Your point of view:**
- What does your industry get wrong that you see clearly?
- What piece of conventional wisdom do you disagree with?
- What's something you had to learn the hard way that most people assume is obvious?
- What frameworks or ways of thinking do you use that others don't?

**Your audience:**
- Who do you want to reach? (Potential clients, hiring managers, peers, a specific industry?)
- What do you want them to think, feel, or do after reading your posts?

**Your voice:**
- Describe your writing style in 3 words
- Link me to any writing you've done (blog posts, articles, emails, case studies) so I can match your tone
- What do you want to avoid sounding like?

## What I need from you

Using everything above, write [NUMBER] LinkedIn posts.

For each post, give me:

1. **The post** — formatted exactly as it would appear on LinkedIn, with blank lines between paragraphs, ready to copy-paste
2. **Format recommendation** — text-only / multi-image / carousel, and why
3. **Hook type** — name which type and why it works for this topic
4. **First comment** — if there's a link or resource to share, write the first comment text
5. **Image note** — if an image would help, describe what to show

**Spread the posts across these content types:**
- At least 2 personal stories (something real that happened)
- At least 2 industry hot takes or contrarian positions (backed by evidence or experience)
- At least 2 educational posts (teach something genuinely useful)
- At least 1 that shares a resource (tool, framework, template, link)
- At least 1 vulnerability post (something you struggled with or got wrong)

**Every post must:**
- Open with a hook under 140 characters that earns the "see more" tap
- End with a question that invites a real response (not a nod)
- Sound like a human who knows their subject, not a content creator who read about it
- Contain at least one specific detail (a number, a name, a place, a date) — generic abstractions kill dwell time
- Create one of these reactions in the reader: "I never thought about it that way", "I've felt exactly that", "That's useful and I'm going to apply it", or "I disagree and want to say so"

**Do not write posts that:**
- Start with "I'm excited to share..."
- Use "game-changer", "revolutionary", "passionate", "journey"
- End with a motivational quote
- Could have been written by anyone in your industry

## A note on tone

The best-performing professional LinkedIn posts in 2026 share a specific quality: they feel like a thoughtful person talking to a colleague, not a brand talking to an audience. They share the messy middle, not just the polished outcome. They admit uncertainty. They sit with an idea instead of wrapping it up neatly. They end with an open question, not a lesson.

Write like that.
